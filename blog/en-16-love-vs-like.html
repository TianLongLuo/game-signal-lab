Love vs. Like: It's Not About Intensity — It's About Direction

2026-08-08 · GAME Signal Lab Blog

You've asked yourself this question before — is this liking or loving? Or the mirror version — do they like me or love me? The reason this question is so hard to answer isn't that the two are similar. It's that we've been taught to distinguish them by degree.

Most people's intuition is: liking is "a little," love is "a lot." Liking is the beginner level; love is the advanced version. But this model is wrong. Liking and loving aren't two points on the same line — they point in entirely different directions.

Liking points toward you. Love points toward them.

The simplest, most accurate diagnostic is: when you're with this person, what are you mostly thinking about?

If you're thinking "how happy I feel," "they make me feel good about myself," "I want more time with them" — that's liking. The core experience of liking is: this person's presence improves my life experience. It faces inward.

If you're thinking "are they happy today," "are they under too much pressure," "what can I do for them" — that's love. The core experience of love is: I want this person's existence to be good, whether or not it involves me. It faces outward.

Liking says "I feel good when I'm with you." Love says "I want you to feel good, even when I'm not there."

This isn't to say that liking is selfish. Liking is healthy and genuine — every relationship begins with "I enjoy being around someone who makes me comfortable." The problem is, many people mistake liking for the final form of love. They believe they deeply love someone, when what they're really doing is enjoying what that person's presence brings to their own experience.

A recordable framework for telling the difference

Discussing "like vs. love" in the abstract is useless — you need concrete observations you can write down. The following questions come from the "Emotional Positioning" module of the GAME review method. Answer them for any relationship, and record the answers in your relationship journal.

  • Direction of giving: In your interactions, is your effort — time, attention, emotional support — primarily aimed at making them happy, or at making yourself feel better inside the relationship?
  • Focus when apart: When they're not around, do you think more about "what are they doing" (curiosity / anxiety), or "are they doing okay" (care / goodwill)?
  • Tolerance for disappointment: When they do something that lets you down, is your first reaction "how could they do this to me," or "why are they like this — can I help"?
  • The relationship's endpoint: If you knew you'd never be together — which hurts more: "I lost someone who made me happy," or "they might not be okay"?
  • Depth of self-disclosure: When you show your vulnerable side to them, is it to receive comfort (liking), or because you trust they can see the real you without running away (love)?

Answer these honestly — not in your head, but on paper. Because when you write it down, you can see the answers you've been dodging.

A common trap: judging by behavior instead of feeling

Many people try to distinguish like from love using behavior: "If they remember my birthday, it's love." "If they'd spend money on me, it's love." The problem with these criteria is that they measure investment level, not emotional direction.

Someone can remember every small thing about you, treat you with extraordinary thoughtfulness, and invest massive amounts of time and money in you — and all of it can still be the ultimate version of liking. Because all of those behaviors can serve a single purpose: keeping you around so they can keep enjoying the experience your presence provides.

The real difference isn't in the behavior itself. It's in the motivation behind it. Same sentence — "wear something warm, don't catch a cold." If the internal monologue is "if you get sick I'll have to take care of you, what a hassle" — that's caring about yourself. If it's "I genuinely don't want you to suffer" — that's caring about you. Identical on the surface. Opposite in direction.

This doesn't mean you need to become a mind-reader. But you can observe a pattern: when your relationship's needs and your own needs conflict, what is their default response? That's the litmus test for direction.

When your happiness and their happiness can't both win, which one does each of you choose? That choice is more honest than any confession.

Liking can become love — but it won't happen automatically

There's good news and less-good news.

The good news: there is indeed a path from liking to love. When you shift from "this person makes me feel good" to "I care about how this person feels, period" — you're walking it. Many long-term relationships have made this transition.

The less-good news: the transition isn't automatic. Plenty of people stay together for years while their relationship model never moves past the "liking" layer — two people mutually meeting each other's needs, providing companionship, but never stepping outside the coordinate system of "how good are you to me." A relationship like this won't necessarily fail. But it has a ceiling: it won't survive a real test.

There's a simple litmus test for whether a relationship has moved from liking to loving: are you willing to do something for them that won't directly benefit you? Not to make them like you more. Not to make the relationship more stable. But simply because you know it'll make their life a little better. If you've never done this — or you have but you're quietly keeping score — your feelings may still be sitting at the liking layer.

Back to your own relationship: what to do now

The point of this article isn't to help you answer "do they love me" — no one can answer that for you. But it can help you do one thing: switch from "guessing mode" to "recording mode."

Today, do this exercise in your relationship journal: list five interactions you had with them over the past week. For each one, answer two questions:

  1. In this interaction, were my actions oriented more toward myself or toward them?
  2. Did I do anything that was "no strings attached" — something that wouldn't make them love me more, but I did it anyway?

Don't rush to a conclusion. Relationship positioning isn't a one-time judgment — it's a process of repeated observation, repeated recording, repeated review. But once you start, you'll notice something: when you stop torturing yourself with "do they love me" and start guiding yourself with "what direction am I heading in," you're already heading toward love.
