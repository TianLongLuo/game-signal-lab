Mixed Signals: Why Someone Can Like You and Still Not Reach Out

2026-08-04 · GAME Signal Lab Blog

If you asked me, "What is the most draining thing about an undefined relationship," my answer would not be rejection, and it would not be arguments. The most draining thing is mixed signals — one day the other person makes you feel valued, the next day it is as if they have completely forgotten you exist. You oscillate between "they are interested" and "don't flatter yourself," pouring enormous energy into guesswork.

This article is not here to help you guess correctly — no one can read another person's mind. What it will do is help you see what mixed signals really are, where they come from, and most importantly: before you make any decision, write the facts down.

Mixed signals are not "playing you" — two forces exist at the same time

Many people jump to an immediate attribution when they encounter mixed signals: "They are keeping options open." "They are using me as a backup." "They are just playing games." These explanations feel satisfying but are usually far from the truth.

The essence of mixed signals is this: the person's attraction to you, and the obstacles preventing them from acting on it, exist simultaneously. These two forces can have completely different origins.

Attraction can come from: enjoying conversations with you, appreciating certain qualities you have, finding you attractive, and even occasionally imagining what a relationship might look like. Obstacles can come from: just ending a previous relationship and not being ready, a career consuming all their energy, fear of commitment, uncertainty about the future of this connection, a personality that is not naturally inclined to initiate, or — and this is very common — their feelings for you simply are not strong enough to overcome their inertia.

Understanding this matters because it pulls you out of the victim narrative of "they are deliberately tormenting me." Most mixed signals are not a calculated strategy. They are the other person also not having figured things out.

Three common mixed-signal patterns

Before anything else, treat these three patterns as factual patterns to observe — not as three puzzles to solve.

Pattern one: warm in person, cold over text. When you are together, the person is engaged, attentive, physically warm, even flirtatious. After you part, replies on messaging apps are slow, they never start a conversation, and they never share about their life. This pattern usually means the person genuinely enjoys being with you in the moment, but lacks the motivation to extend the connection into everyday life.

Pattern two: they appear when they need you, vanish when they do not. The person reaches out when they are feeling low or need help. You respond seriously. Then they disappear. This is not just "they only think of me when they are vulnerable" — it is that your presence satisfies a specific need of theirs, but the other side of that need (day-to-day companionship, long-term commitment) is not something they want you to fill.

Pattern three: they express affection but avoid moving forward. The person tells you they enjoy being with you, calls you special, even actively asks you out. But the moment you try to advance the relationship — talk about the future, define the relationship, meet friends — they pull back immediately. This usually points to a fear of intimacy, or deep uncertainty about what they themselves want.

The common thread across all three is this: the affection is real, but the conditions for forward movement are not met. Affection is the spark; action is the fuel. Without enough fuel, even the brightest spark will not catch fire.

How to replace guesswork with record-keeping

Mixed signals are agonizing because your brain switches between two narratives — "they clearly like me" and "they do not care at all" — and you can find evidence for either in some specific behavior. The problem is that the evidence you pick tends to be the one your current emotion wants.

The solution: before guessing, record. Give yourself two weeks. List every "positive signal" and "negative signal" from the other person, with no interpretation — just facts.

In the positive column: "invited me to dinner once," "remembered my birthday," "asked three genuine questions about me in the last conversation." In the negative column: "took a full day to reply three times," "canceled plans twice," "has never once shared about their daily life."

After two weeks, lay both columns out and look. Do not ask which column has more items — quantity is meaningless. Ask whether these facts form a consistent pattern. If the negative signals all point in the same direction (e.g., the person consistently avoids moving forward) while the positive signals all point in another (e.g., they are engaged when physically together), that contradiction itself is the most important finding.

Three questions to ask yourself

After observing and recording, the decision is not made by the other person's signals — it is made by your own needs and boundaries. Ask yourself three things:

First: what kind of relationship do you actually want? If the answer is "a committed, mutually invested long-term relationship," then a person who only gives you warmth in the moment but no promises about the future cannot meet your needs — no matter how genuine their affection might be.

Second: how much longer can you tolerate this uncertainty? Do not lie to yourself with "I can wait forever" — everyone has a limit. Set a time window for yourself. If nothing changes by the end of it, choose to walk away with dignity.

Third: if this person never changes — if they are only good to you in person, never initiate, never move the relationship forward — would you still keep investing? You do not have to answer this question immediately. But the answer contains your most honest assessment of this person.

Signals are for observation, not negotiation. You cannot take the twenty pieces of evidence you have recorded that "they might like me" and use them to convince someone who does not take initiative to change. You can only use those records to convince yourself to make a clear-eyed decision.

Finally: accepting "no answer" as an answer

Some mixed signals last for months and end with one side walking away. But more often, the ending is not that the other person finally figured things out — it is that you did. You do not need to wait for their answer before deciding what you want.
