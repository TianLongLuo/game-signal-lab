Signal Density in Texting: Response Speed, Content Quality, and Initiation

2026-08-04 · GAME Signal Lab Blog

Most modern relationships start with text. On our side of the screen, we often spend the minutes between messages analyzing response speed, punctuation, tone, and emoji choices. But the thing truly worth watching in a text conversation is not any single message — it is the signal density: how much meaningful information the other person actually transmits over time.

The facts: three dimensions you can record

Signal density has three observable dimensions, and all of them are facts you can write down without any guessing.

First, response speed. How quickly does the person usually reply, is that speed stable, and does it change noticeably over time? Second, content quality. Are replies lazy one-liners like "haha" or "okay," or do they continue the thread? Does the person remember small things you mentioned and ask real questions? Third, initiation. On days when you do not reach out, does the person start conversations on their own — and what share of your total exchanges are initiated by them?

Writing these down is how you turn feelings into evidence. Feelings lie; records do not.

Interpretation: how to read each dimension

Response speed should be read against a baseline, not as an absolute number. Someone who replies instantly to everyone is not showing you anything special. Meanwhile, a busy person who normally replies a day later might be showing real care by replying the same evening. What matters is fluctuation: noticeably faster with you than with their usual pace — or the reverse, fast at first and slower over time. Speed alone is the easiest thing to misread.

Content quality is far more reliable than speed. A person can reply late at night, yet remember what you mentioned last week, follow your thread with questions, and share fragments of their own life. That quality cannot be explained by "they happened to be free." On the other hand, quick replies that are always "lol," "okay," or "haha" suggest someone completing the act of responding rather than having a conversation with you.

Initiation is the rarest signal — but look at what they initiate. Texting you about their day, sending you a song, asking how you are: that is genuine willingness to connect. But if the only things they initiate are favors, or one-sided emotional dumping with no interest in your response, that is using you, not caring about you. The content of an initiation matters as much as its frequency.

The method: a one-week window, not a single day

The practice is simple: give yourself an observation window — say, seven days. Each day, spend five minutes writing down one fact per dimension: only what happened, no commentary. When you review at the end of the week, patterns will be clearer than any single message — you will see whether the other person's signal density is stable, rising, or falling.

Finally: density is not a score

Signal density is not an exam grade. No threshold has to be reached to prove someone cares. It is simply a mirror that reflects the real rhythm of a relationship. After a few weeks of recording, you usually stop needing to ask, "Do they actually like me?" — the answer is already in the pattern. All you have to do is trust the evidence time provides, and move at your own pace from there.
